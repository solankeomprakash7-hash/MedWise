Your senior asks, "ECG normal?"
You glance at it. / Nothing jumps out.
So you say yes.
[pause]
Could you DEFEND that?

Don't panic.
An ECG is normal when it passes nine checks.
Here's how to do each one, / and why.

First, the grid.
One small box is 40 milliseconds.
One big box, / five small ones, / is 200.


ONE. Rate.
Count the big boxes between two R waves /
and divide 300 by that number.
Three boxes, / 100.
Five boxes, / 60.
So for a normal rate, /
the R waves sit three to five big boxes apart.
Irregular rhythm? / Count the QRS complexes on the 10-second strip /
and multiply by six.

TWO. Is it sinus?
A normal beat starts in the SA node /
and travels down and to the left, / toward lead II.
So in lead II, / the P wave points UP.
Then look for a P before every QRS, /
a QRS after every P, /
and the same gap between them, / every beat.
No P waves and an irregularly irregular rhythm? /
Think atrial fibrillation.

THREE. Axis.
That's the direction the electricity takes through the ventricles.
Normally, / down and to the left, / toward the big left ventricle.
Lead I looks from the left.
aVF looks from below.
QRS mostly upright in both? /
The current is heading toward both. / Normal axis.
aVF negative? / Don't panic yet.
Check lead II.
II still positive? / Still normal.
II negative too? / That's left axis deviation.

FOUR. The PR interval.
Start of the P / to the start of the QRS.
That's the AV node holding the impulse back for a moment /
so the atria can finish filling the ventricles.
Normal: / three to five small boxes, / 120 to 200 milliseconds.
Longer than five? / First-degree heart block.
Shorter than three? / Think of a shortcut pathway, / like WPW.

FIVE. QRS width.
This is the ventricles firing.
The conduction system spreads the impulse so FAST /
that the QRS stays under three small boxes. / 120 milliseconds.
Wider means the impulse took a slower route: /
a bundle branch block, / a beat that started in the ventricle, /
or pre-excitation.

[END OF PART 4A]
That's five of the nine.
The last four are in the next Reel, /
including the one where MIs get missed.
Follow so you don't miss it.

[START OF PART 4B]
Part 4, continued.
We've done rate, rhythm, axis, PR and QRS.
Four checks left / before you can call an ECG normal.


SIX. R wave progression.
V1 sits over the right side of the heart, / V6 over the left.
Moving toward the left ventricle, /
the R wave gets TALLER / and the S wave smaller.
Around V3 or V4 they're about equal. / That's the transition.
If the R waves don't grow, /
think of an old anterior MI, / LVH, /
or electrodes placed in the wrong spot.

SEVEN. The ST segment.
Right after the QRS, / every ventricular cell is at the same voltage.
No current flows, / so the ST segment is flat, /
level with the baseline before the P wave.
Check it in EVERY lead.
If it's raised in two leads that look at the same wall, /
or pushed down, /
this ECG is not normal, / however good the rest looks.

EIGHT. T waves.
The T wave is the ventricles recovering.
It runs the opposite way with the opposite charge, /
so the T points the same way as the QRS.
It should be upright in I, II, / and V3 to V6.
In aVR, / it's ALWAYS upside down.
And on its own, / an inverted T in III, aVL, aVF or V1 /
can still be normal.

NINE. The QT.
Start of the QRS / to the end of the T.
It shortens as the heart speeds up, / so correct it for rate: / the QTc.
Normal is under 450 in men, / 460 in women.
Why care? / A long QT can set off a dangerous rhythm /
called torsades.


Two more things that scare beginners /
and are completely normal.
Small q waves in I, aVL, V5 and V6.
That's the septum firing first, / from left to right.
And a heart rate that speeds up as the patient breathes in /
and slows as they breathe out.
Sinus arrhythmia. / Normal, / especially in young people.


All nine pass? /
Then say "normal ECG" /
and MEAN it.

And if there's an old ECG, /
put them side by side.
Something that's changed since last time /
matters more than any single number.
